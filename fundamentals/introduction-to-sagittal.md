# Introduction to Sagittal

Welcome to Sagittal, the universal system for pitch notation. This page is a brief introduction — the who, what, and why. Don't worry about memorizing anything yet; just get a feel for the shape of the thing.

## The name

The name "Sagittal" comes from the Latin *sagitta*, "arrow" — because each Sagittal symbol looks like some kind of arrow. George Secor took it from Ivor Darreg's phrase "sagittarian notation," and you can keep the friendlier image of **Sagittarius, the archer** in mind as a mnemonic. Just remember it is the *arrow*, not the archer, that gives Sagittal its name. (The [origin story](../history/the-origin-of-sagittal.md) has the full account.)

It is spelled with one "g" and two "t's". If you ever forget, remember: **Sagittal is unforgettable.**

For short, Sagittal symbols can simply be called **sagittals**, with a lowercase "s". Each sagittal, like a specialized sharp or flat, indicates a slight alteration in pitch — either up or down.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Is Sagittal too complicated?** There are over a hundred Sagittal symbols, but most people never have to use most of them. These three alone cover maybe 80% of use cases, and you won't even see 90% of the rest until the advanced material. So: don't freak out.
{% endhint %}

We call these three symbols the Sagittal **starter set**.

## Mirrored pairs

Each sagittal is a member of a pair: one up, and one down, altering pitch by the same amount but in the opposite direction. The up and down versions are not 180° rotations of each other — they are **vertical mirrors**.

This mirrored pairing does two good things:

* **Clear direction.** An arrow always shows plainly which way the pitch moves. Contrast the conventional sharp and flat: if those two symbols weren't already second nature to you, it would be a bit hard to tell which was up and which was down.
* **Half as much to learn.** Because every downward symbol is just the mirror of an upward one, the mirrored-pair principle effectively halves the number of new symbols you need to learn.

Sagittal does not ask you to abandon the conventional sharp and flat — those are firmly entrenched in common practice, and Sagittal is happy to work with them.

## Size and position

Notice that sagittals are not as tall as the sharp or flat. That lets them stack better in chords.

And, like the flat symbol, the shaft is only there to give direction. **The staff position the symbol refers to is where the strokes are thick** — the arrowhead. (This becomes an engraving rule later: you center the arrowhead on the staff position, just as you center a flat's loop.)

## Size-to-pitch-alteration proportionality

Sorted by increasing size, the starter set is about **22¢, 27¢, and 53¢** — where a cent is a hundredth of a 12-EDO semitone.

Sagittal symbols exhibit **size-to-pitch-alteration proportionality**: the bigger the shaded area of the symbol, the larger the pitch alteration it represents. In the downward case you can think of it as how much water the symbol could hold. The benefit is real: even if you momentarily forget a symbol while sight-reading, its visual size tells you roughly how much to adjust.

In rough terms, these three sagittals are about a fifth of a sharp, a quarter of a sharp, and half a sharp. So the first two make handy quarter-tone symbols, and all of them are smaller than one step of conventional tuning — in a word, they are microtonal.

## From cents to commas

Why 22, 27, and 53 cents? Those numbers aren't special. Cents are just measurements that help us compare sizes; with more decimal places these intervals are closer to 21.5¢, 27.3¢, and 53.3¢, and we could keep going forever.

The real information is not the cents. It is the **justly intoned frequency ratios** those cents are approximating, and those can be stated simply and exactly. These small JI intervals are called **commas**, and each up/down pair of sagittals is associated with one comma:

| Symbol | Comma | Name | Prime |
|---|---|---|---|
| `/|` | 80:81 | syntonic comma | 5 |
| `|)` | 63:64 | septimal comma | 7 |
| `/|\` | 32:33 | undecimal comma | 11 |

(The up symbol puts the comma's larger number on top; the down symbol puts it on the bottom.) These are the most important commas for notating the prime limits 5, 7, and 11 — so with just these three sagittals you could, for example, notate an 11-odd-limit tonality diamond.

## Primary commas and flexible values

These commas are not the *only* values these sagittals can represent. They are the **default** value — unless a score says otherwise, you should assume that's what a symbol means — so we call each a sagittal's **primary comma**.

Having flexible values is a big part of what makes Sagittal special. It is what lets a single Sagittal score be interpreted across different tunings. For example, those same three symbols happen to notate the first three steps of **72-EDO**, one of the most popular and useful EDOs. You can simply memorize the sagittals for your favorite EDO — which symbol is 1 step, which is 2 steps, and so on — and if you go a little further and learn the primary commas, the notation quietly tells you something about how that EDO shapes harmonic space.

## System, collection, notation

So Sagittal is more than a single collection of symbols forming a single notation (the model of one-off notations built for one tuning, like Ben Johnston notation for extended JI, or Ezra Sims notation for 72-EDO). It is even more than a collection of many notations drawn from a shared set of symbols.

It is most accurately called a **pitch notation system**, where the flexible values of the symbols give rise to an open-ended network of related, compatible tuning notations:

> **system > collection > notation**

Not only does every tuning have a Sagittal notation — every tuning has potentially *many* Sagittal notations, each valid and all compatible. New specialized notations, for regular temperaments or non-octave tunings, are being developed all the time. The goal of Sagittal is nothing less than to be the last pitch notation system the universe will ever need.

## Not a new idea

Symbol flexibility is powerful, but it is not new. Consider the sharp. We could say it represents one step of 12-EDO — but that is only one of its many *secondary* values. Its original meaning is the interval required to move from a lettered pitch past all the other letters once and back to itself, offset by a bit: seven pure fifths, octave-reduced, which works out to the ratio 2187/2048, the **chromatic semitone**. That is about 114 cents, not exactly 100.

The sharp doesn't mean any *one* of its values. What it means is: anything sufficiently similar to moving by a chromatic semitone, in whichever musical context. All sagittals follow the same pattern. Sagittal didn't pioneer an epic new approach to pitch notation; it took an existing good idea — symbol flexibility — and expanded on it with ingenuity and care.

## Who made it

Sagittal notation was created by **Dave Keenan and George Secor** in the early 2000s, with significant input from members of the alternative-tuning communities then hosted on Yahoo Groups. It was formally introduced to the world in **Volume 18 (2006)** of the historic journal *Xenharmonikôn*, in an article which discusses and defends its design choices.
